## Distinction and recommended framing

**Planned homebirth** includes qualified clinical attendance, antenatal suitability assessment and coordinated access to hospital care. **Freebirth/unassisted birth** means choosing birth without a midwife or other qualified maternity professional. A companion/doula does not replace qualified care. Do not apply evidence from attended planned homebirth to freebirth. Recommend qualified maternity care prominently, discuss added risks from unrecognized complications or delayed treatment, and support continued antenatal/postnatal care or changing the plan without judgment. No clinical birth management, procedures, medication dosing, resuscitation or cord/placenta instructions.

Suggested card text:

> Planned homebirth includes qualified maternity care and a plan for hospital transfer. Ask your maternity team whether it suits your pregnancy, who will attend, and how help can reach you. Your care team can explain your personal risks and local services.

> Freebirth means giving birth without a midwife or other qualified maternity professional. Qualified care is strongly recommended. Complications can arise unexpectedly, and recognizing them or getting treatment may be delayed without clinical attendance, adding risks for you and your baby. You can discuss your preferences, keep antenatal and postnatal care, and change your plan at any time. After an unattended birth, contact maternity services promptly for checks for you and your baby.

No outcome statistics are needed in the app. The evidence does not establish a precise isolated freebirth risk multiple.

NHS recommends speaking with a midwife for informed choices/personalized support, and explains that a midwife checks health, identifies a need for help and arranges medical attention. ### NICE NG235: Intrapartum care — HTTP 200

https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng235/chapter/Recommendations

Fetched 2 October 2026. Raw `nice_ng235.txt` / `.html`. Clinician-facing UK guidance.

Exact excerpts:

- §1.1.1: “how to contact their midwifery care team and what to do in an emergency.”
- §1.2.2: “Ensure that all women giving birth have timely access to an obstetric unit if they need transfer of care”
- §1.3.5: “the reasons why this might happen, the time it may take, the delay in obstetric or neonatal care this may cause, and how her birth companion will travel”
- §1.5.4: “Carry out transfer of care of women in labour as soon as possible after the decision to transfer has been made.”

Supports suitability review, qualified attendance, access to care, local transfer times/destination, reasons for transfer, companion travel, clear handover and prompt clinical-team-arranged transfer. Transfer may be needed for labour concerns or a request for pain relief; it is not always a life-threatening emergency. Discuss both routine/urgent and emergency routes in advance; follow clinical/dispatcher directions.

### NHS: Signs that labour has begun — HTTP 200

https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/labour-and-birth/signs-that-labour-has-begun/

Fetched 2 October 2026; older signs-of-labour URL redirects here. Raw `nhs_labour.txt` / `.html`.

Exact excerpts:

- “Do not wait until the next day – call immediately, even if it's the middle of the night.”
- “you think your baby is coming now and you have a strong urge to push” (under emergency ambulance action)

Call maternity team for suspected labour or worry. Urgent contact: waters breaking, vaginal bleeding, reduced movement, suspected labour before 37 weeks, contractions lasting over 2 minutes or 6+ contractions in 10 minutes. Emergency help if birth seems imminent/strong urge to push. A timer does not diagnose labour or decide when care is safe to delay.

### NICE NG194: Postnatal care — HTTP 200

https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng194/chapter/Recommendations

Fetched 2 October 2026. Raw `nice_ng194.txt` / `.html`.

Exact excerpts:

- §1.1.14: “Ensure that the first postnatal visit by a midwife takes place within 36 hours after transfer of care from the place of birth or after a home birth.”
- §1.3.3: “Carry out a complete examination of the baby within 72 hours of the birth and at 6 to 8 weeks after the birth”
- §1.3.4: “Offer parents vitamin K prophylaxis for their babies to prevent vitamin K deficiency bleeding”

Arrange parent/baby assessment, face-to-face postnatal midwife visit, newborn physical examination, vitamin K discussion, blood-spot/hearing screening, feeding support and safer-sleep guidance. 36h/72h/6–8-week timing is UK guidance; prompt local scheduling in a global app. Freebirth aftercare should seek maternity services promptly rather than waiting for a routine visit. Parent warning signs requiring advice without delay (§1.2.4): sudden/heavy/increasing bleeding; fever/shivering or severe/worsening abdominal/pelvic/perineal pain/foul discharge; leg swelling/tenderness; breathlessness/chest pain; persistent/severe headache.

### NHS: Urgent medical help for babies/children under 5 — HTTP 200

https://www.nhs.uk/baby/health/when-to-get-urgent-medical-help-for-babies-and-children-under-5/

Fetched 2 October 2026; page reviewed 6 August 2026. Raw `nhs_newborn.txt` / `.html`.

Exact excerpts: “has fainted, is difficult to wake, or cannot be woken”; “is breathing very fast or has difficulty breathing”; “is a baby and is not feeding”; “They do not need to have every symptom to be seriously unwell.”

Immediate medical help/emergency care for serious breathing difficulty, blue/grey/pale/mottled appearance, difficult waking/unresponsiveness, seizure, non-fading rash, temperature 38°C+ under 3 months or below 36°C, or no urine in 12h (NHS immediate-action list). Absent feeding is a serious-illness sign needing prompt urgent advice; do not automatically classify every missed feed as ambulance-level. Do not wait for all signs.

## Practical checklist content

**Planned homebirth**

1. Review suitability and personal risks with a qualified maternity professional.
2. Confirm attending team, on-call/backup arrangements and maternity contact.
3. Agree when to call and review urgent/emergency warning signs.
4. Record agreed transfer destination, local expected times and who arranges transfer; distinguish clinical-team-coordinated transfer from calling emergency services for an emergency.
5. Keep phone charged, full address/location and access instructions ready; arrange companion travel/childcare/pet care if needed (logistical suggestions).
6. Keep records, birth preferences, medicines list and transfer bag accessible.
7. Ask the team about practical home setup/pool arrangements and what they provide.
8. Arrange postnatal parent/baby care, feeding support and newborn checks/screening.

**Considering or planning freebirth**

1. Discuss preferences/concerns and care adaptations with a qualified professional; review attended homebirth, birth-centre and hospital options.
2. Keep/arrange antenatal care and an individual risk review.
3. Save maternity, urgent-care and local emergency contacts; phone charged; address/access information ready.
4. Have transfer destination/access/records/transfer-bag plan and discuss urgent signs with a support person.
5. Arrange prompt clinical parent/baby checks and continued postnatal care, physical examination, vitamin K discussion, feeding support and screening.
6. Check local birth notification/registration requirements with official services.
7. Know that qualified support can be requested and the plan changed at any time.

These are care-access and contingency items, not a clinical birth kit or proof that unassisted birth is safe. Localize service names and numbers; do not hardcode UK 999/111 or US 911 for every user. Follow call-handler instructions in an emergency.
